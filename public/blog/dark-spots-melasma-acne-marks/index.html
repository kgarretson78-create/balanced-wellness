Dark Spots, Melasma, or Acne Marks? Understanding Uneven Skin Tone in Kingsport and Jonesborough

You notice a brown patch in a photograph, a mark that remains after a breakout, or a cluster of spots that seems more visible than last year. It is tempting to group them all under “sun damage” and start looking for a treatment. But similar-looking discoloration can have different causes, and that distinction matters.

For anyone exploring aesthetic care in Kingsport or Jonesborough, Tennessee, understanding the concern comes before choosing a procedure. At Balanced Wellness Medical Spa, a consultation is an opportunity to discuss your goals, review your skin history, and determine whether aesthetic treatment or evaluation by a dermatologist is the appropriate next step.

Dark spots describe an appearance, not one diagnosis

Hyperpigmentation describes areas that look darker because of increased pigment. That broad description can include several conditions. The American Academy of Dermatology explains that inflammation, injury, irritating products, certain medications, and hormonal changes can all contribute to discoloration.

Instead of arriving with a diagnosis from social media, bring a history. When did you first notice the area? Did it follow a breakout, rash, product change, or procedure? Has it changed? Those details are more useful than deciding in advance that a particular facial or device must be the answer.

Sun-related spots and melasma need different conversations

Age spots, also called solar lentigines, are often associated with sun exposure and may resemble larger freckles. However, appearance alone cannot establish that a spot is harmless. The American Academy of Dermatology recommends a dermatologist’s assessment before treating presumed age spots, because other conditions, including skin cancer, can look similar.

Melasma is another cause of darker patches. Sunlight and hormonal factors can play a role, and it can persist or recur. According to the American Academy of Dermatology, treatment is individualized and often begins with sun protection and topical medication; selected procedures may sometimes be added by a dermatologist.

The practical implication is simple: a treatment appropriate for one diagnosed pigment concern may not suit another. If you have been told you have melasma, share that diagnosis before discussing light-based procedures. Ask whether your plan should be coordinated with a board-certified dermatologist.

A flat mark after acne is different from a scar

A breakout may settle while leaving a flat, darker mark behind. This can be post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. It is different from a change in skin texture, such as an indentation or raised scar. You can also have discoloration and a textural change in the same area.

For pigmentation following acne or eczema, controlling the underlying condition matters. Otherwise, new marks can continue appearing while older ones fade. Explain whether you are still having breakouts or flares, and bring the names of prescribed treatments. Do not stop a medication because you suspect it affects pigmentation; discuss that concern with your prescriber.

What this means for laser and IPL consultations

Balanced offers laser skin consultations. Ask which options are available at your preferred location and suitable for your skin. These are options to discuss after your concern has been assessed. The presence of a treatment on a menu does not establish that it is appropriate for every patch or skin tone.

The American Academy of Dermatology notes that laser and light treatments can address selected pigment concerns, but darker skin is more prone to burns and dark marks following laser treatment. Provider experience with your skin tone and the specific technology matters. Share any history of pigment changes after previous procedures.

Ask your provider to name the proposed treatment and explain why it fits your diagnosed concern. Useful questions include: What improvement is realistic? Could the treatment worsen pigmentation? What alternatives should I consider? What preparation and aftercare will I need? A clear explanation should help you understand the decision before committing.

Sun protection belongs in a pigment-care plan

Sun protection supports treatment and ongoing care. For melasma, the American Academy of Dermatology recommends broad-spectrum, water-resistant sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher, along with shade and protective clothing. A tinted sunscreen containing iron oxide can also help protect against visible light, which may worsen melasma, particularly in darker skin tones.

Choose a formulation you can comfortably use, and follow application and reapplication directions. Bring questions about texture, sensitivity, or a visible cast to your clinician. A sunscreen that works with your routine is easier to use consistently than one that stays unopened in a drawer.

Give your skin a manageable routine

A shelf full of brightening products can make it difficult to tell what helps and what irritates. The American Academy of Dermatology advises gentle products for people with melasma, because irritation may darken spots. More frequent exfoliation is not automatically a better approach.

Before adding another product, list what you already use and how often. Include cleansers, exfoliating acids, retinoids, spot treatments, and prescription creams. Ask which items have a purpose in your plan, which should be paused or adjusted under professional guidance, and how you will know whether your skin is tolerating the routine.

Know when medical evaluation comes first

A new, changing, bleeding, or nonhealing spot deserves medical attention. The CDC identifies a new growth, a sore that does not heal, or a change in a mole as possible skin cancer signs. Seek evaluation rather than trying to lighten or cover the area with an aesthetic procedure.

For an established cosmetic concern, discuss how progress will be assessed. Photographs in similar lighting can make follow-up conversations more useful. Ask when reassessment makes sense and what would prompt a change in plan. Improvement can take time, and no treatment can promise permanently even skin.
